The Quansight summer internship program is back for another year. We are excited to introduce our six interns for 2026,
and to follow them on their journeys.

Every year, Quansight runs this [internship program]() to give new open source talent a chance to grow. Interns work side by side with Quansight mentors, learning how open source projects work, how to make impactful contributions, and how to best collaborate with the scientific Python ecosystem.

Here is a look at this year's cohort and their projects.

## Aaishwarya Mishra

<div style={{display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center'}}>
<img alt="A golden retriever wearing a carved watermelon helmet." src="/posts/2026-interns/AaishwaryaMishra.jpeg" width="220px" style={{padding: '10px 20px'}}/>

[Aaishwarya](https://github.com/aaishwarymishra) joins us from India and is working on a PyTorch-Ignite Training
Toolkit, mentored by Victor Fomin (of OpenTeams). The project runs from May to August.

[PyTorch-Ignite](https://pytorch-ignite.ai/) is a tool that helps people train and test neural networks. It takes away a
lot of repeated work so researchers can focus on their ideas. Aaishwarya is building Trainite, a toolbox for training
language models. The goals are to train and test a basic transformer model, customize parts of the training process, and
write clear documentation so others can use the work.

Aaishwarya is interested in AI and PyData, and strongly believes that, just like software, AI should be open and
accessible to everyone. 

## Iason Krommydas

<div style={{display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center'}}>
<img alt="Iason Krommydas smiling at the camera." src="/posts/2026-interns/IasonKrommydas.JPG" width="220px" style={{padding: '10px 20px'}}/>

[Iason](https://github.com/ikrommyd) joins us from Texas by way of Greece and is working on NumPy gufuncs with mentor
Nathan Goldbaum. This project runs from June to August.

NumPy uses something called [ufuncs](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/ufuncs.html) to do fast math on arrays.
[Gufuncs](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/c-api/generalized-ufuncs.html) are a more flexible version of this,
built for more complex operations. Iason is looking for new ways to use gufuncs inside NumPy. This project is adding
support for reductions with more than one result, building a new minmax reduction, implementing segmented reductions,
and converting NumPy operations into gufuncs to add more features and for performance. If you follow the
[NumPy repository](https://github.com/numpy/numpy), you may have already seen this work show up.

Iason is a PhD student at Rice University, conducting research in high-energy physics as a member of the
[CMS experiment](https://cms.cern) at the
[Large Hadron Collider at CERN](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/large-hadron-collider/), focusing on Higgs boson
decays into two photons. He's passionate about scientific computing and open-source tools that enable reproducible and
efficient research. He has maintained several tools of the [Scikit-HEP](https://scikit-hep.org/) stack like
[Awkward Array](https://awkward-array.org/doc/main/) and [Coffea](https://coffea-hep.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) and is a
contributor to many packages across the stack. Outside of work, he enjoys watching movies, gaming, and travelling.

## Yu Sheng Aow

<div style={{display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center'}}>
<img alt="A side profile picture of Yu Sheng." src="/posts/2026-interns/YuSheng.png" width="220px" style={{padding: '10px 20px'}}/>

[Yu Sheng](https://github.com/yusheng036) joins us from Boston by way of Malaysia and is working on
[finch-tensor](https://github.com/finch-tensor/finch-tensor), a sparse tensor programming system, under the mentorship
of Mateusz Sokół. This project runs from June to August.

Finch is a compiler that generates efficient code for sparse array and tensor programs. The compiler allows users to
write simple, readable code without giving up speed. Yu Sheng is adding support for a brand new backend for the Finch
compiler. This work reaches beyond our usual community, as Finch is also part of ongoing research at several
universities. The goals include handwritten kernels to show the feasibility of the backend, building a full prototype,
and benchmarking against existing backends.

Yu Sheng got his first break contributing to open source by joining the Finch team. He would like to contribute to more
open-source repositories in the future and make changes that matter.

Outside of work, Yu Sheng has a big interest in swimming, including open-water swimming. He also enjoys playing tennis
and sailing.

## Aadya Chinubhai

<div style={{display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center'}}>

[Aadya](https://github.com/aadya940) joins us from California by way of India and is working on approximating data with
splines, mentored by Gagandeep Singh and Evgeni Burovski. This project runs from June to September.

[Splines](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/tutorial/interpolate/splines_and_polynomials.html) are a way to build smooth
curves through data. Many tools in the PyData ecosystem depend on [SciPy](https://scipy.org/) for this kind of work, but
SciPy has lagged behind other tools in this area. Aadya is helping close that gap. This project includes building
smoothing splines with set knots, working on regression splines with different penalty methods, and exploring new ways
to handle regularization.

Aadya got started with open source through Google Summer of Code and later became a maintainer of
[aeon](https://github.com/aeon-toolkit/aeon), a toolkit for time series machine learning. His professional interests
include Python, systems programming, machine learning, mathematics, and building AI agents.

Outside of work and research, he enjoys painting, cooking, jogging, the gym, going to hackathons and of course coffee +
coding. He also played soccer competitively for around 10 years and had the opportunity to represent Gujarat in a
national-level tournament. He enjoys spending time outdoors, especially at beaches and on short hikes.

## Aditi Juneja

<div style={{display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center'}}>
<img alt="Colorful geometric pattern made of lines within a square." src="/posts/2026-interns/AditiJuneja.png" width="220px" style={{padding: '10px 20px'}}/>

[Aditi (@Schefflera-Arboricola)](https://github.com/Schefflera-Arboricola) joins us from India and is working on
optimizing Sphinx builds, mentored by Agriya Khetarpal and Melissa Mendonça. This project runs from July to October.

[Sphinx](https://www.sphinx-doc.org/en/master/) is the tool that builds documentation for many Python projects,
including CPython, NumPy, SciPy, PyTorch, and even the Linux kernel. On big projects, a full build can take several
minutes, and there has not been an easy way to see where that time goes. Aditi is investigating tools to measure and
speed up Sphinx builds. Faster builds mean less time waiting and less computing power used across every project that
relies on Sphinx.

Aditi has mostly been contributing to the Scientific Python ecosystem (mostly, dispatching mechanisms), maintaining
[NetworkX](https://networkx.org/en/), the [nx-parallel backend](https://github.com/Schefflera-Arboricola/nx-parallel),
including a few contributions to [scikit-image](https://scikit-image.org/) as well. She has worked on improving
performance, benchmarking, interoperability, packaging and general maintenance stuff.

## Pratham Hole

<div style={{display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center'}}>
<img alt="Pratham looking at the camera." src="/posts/2026-interns/PrathamHole.jpg" width="220px" style={{padding: '10px 20px'}}/>

[Pratham](https://github.com/prathamhole14) also joins us from India for a July to October project mentored by Matti
Picus. He is working toward two connected goals: porting NumPy toward CPython's Limited C API, and moving NumPy closer
to supporting sub-interpreters.

Both push against the same underlying constraint, which is how tightly NumPy is currently bound to one CPython, at one
version, in one process. The Limited C API is the slice of the C API backed by the stable ABI, and building against it
yields a single abi3 wheel that survives new CPython releases instead of needing a rebuild for each one.
[Sub-interpreters](https://docs.python.org/3/c-api/subinterpreters.html) press on the other half of the problem. They
are isolated Python interpreters sharing a single process, and since Python 3.12 each can run with its own GIL, and so
in true parallel. NumPy blocks that import today rather than misbehave quietly, because its data still lives in C
globals that every interpreter would end up sharing, which is precisely the isolation sub-interpreters depend on.

The two goals therefore need much the same groundwork: avoiding APIs outside the limited subset, replacing static types
with heap types, and lifting mutable global state into per-module state.

Professionally, Pratham is interested in ML, its tools, and backend development, and is currently spending his time
exploring the CPython C API. Outside of software engineering, he is quite a cinephile, loves going for long walks to
unwind, and is always on the lookout for good food.

This year, the program has a bit more flexibility than usual. Some interns started earlier, and some later, depending on
what worked for each person. Projects run for about three months, and at the end, each intern writes a blog post about
their work and their experience. [These posts](https://labs.quansight.org/categories/internship/) are some of our
favorite reads each year, and are a good opportunity for interns to present their projects to a wider audience.

In the following weeks, you can expect to read more in this series of blog posts.

A big thank you goes out to every mentor who is giving their time this year, and to the community contributors reviewing
and interacting with our interns. We are proud of this cohort already, and we cannot wait to see what they build!
